Research

As long as I can remember, I always wanted to be a researcher.

Research interests

My work sits where aerosol physics, sensing hardware and data science meet, and it is aimed at one practical end: particulate matter measurements that people can act on. Eight threads run through it.

  • Sensor characterisation and validation in controlled environments
  • Machine learning for sensor calibration
  • Meteorological data integration and prediction
  • Aerosol source apportionment and factor analysis
  • Urban IoT air quality monitoring networks
  • Spatiotemporal analysis of particulate matter, with an India focus
  • Emission control technologies, industrial and indoor
  • Human exposure and health impact assessment

They are deliberately linked rather than parallel. A chamber result changes the calibration model; the calibration model decides whether a network’s spatial field is real; and only a field you can trust supports an exposure estimate or a policy claim.

From sparse networks to reliable PM2.5

I have designed and commissioned a 1.3 m³ environmental chamber for controlled sensor evaluation, co-located and calibrated sensor networks across Delhi–NCR and the Indo-Gangetic Plain, built physical-optical forward models from Mie scattering and κ–Köhler theory, and used satellite reanalysis products such as MERRA-2 as calibration proxies where no reference monitor exists.

These four strands — field, laboratory, physical and satellite — converge on one idea: the bias in a low-cost sensor is not noise to be regressed away but a physical signature of the aerosol it is measuring. Blending the evidence and removing the bias that physics can explain leaves a correction that travels across a network, rather than one that expires at the co-location site.

More broadly, I am interested in frameworks that connect measurement, source attribution and exposure, so that a dense network reports not only how much particulate matter is present but where it came from and who is breathing it, with a stated and verified uncertainty rather than an assumed one.

Prior to my PhD, I completed my Master’s in Environmental Science and Engineering at IIT Bombay, where I worked on the techno-economic assessment of control technologies for capturing particulate matter from industrial and indoor sources.

Working across the chamber, the field and the model led me to the question that continues to guide my work: what does it actually take to make an inexpensive measurement trustworthy enough to act on, and how far can that trust be carried beyond the place it was established?

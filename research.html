Five threads, one question: when can you believe a cheap sensor?

Research interests

My work sits where aerosol physics, sensing hardware and data science meet, and it is aimed at one practical end: particulate matter measurements that people can act on. Eight threads run through it.

  • Sensor characterisation and validation in controlled environments
  • Machine learning for sensor calibration
  • Meteorological data integration and prediction
  • Aerosol source apportionment and factor analysis
  • Urban IoT air quality monitoring networks
  • Spatiotemporal analysis of particulate matter, with an India focus
  • Emission control technologies, industrial and indoor
  • Human exposure and health impact assessment

They are deliberately linked rather than parallel. A chamber result changes the calibration model; the calibration model decides whether a network’s spatial field is real; and only a field you can trust supports an exposure estimate or a policy claim.

From sparse networks to reliable PM2.5

Low-cost sensors promise dense coverage, but two things stand in the way: reference networks are too sparse to calibrate against everywhere, and the sensors respond to particle composition, size distribution and humidity rather than to mass alone. My doctoral work attacks the problem from four directions at once.

  • Field evaluation — co-location against reference monitors across real source mixes, quantifying how much performance actually varies in the world.
  • Laboratory assessment — controlled exposures to different aerosol matrices with independent temperature and humidity control, isolating bias specific to particle type.
  • Physics-based modelling — Mie scattering and hygroscopic growth corrections that explain the bias from optical first principles instead of absorbing it into a fitted constant.
  • Scalable calibration — reanalysis and satellite proxies such as MERRA-2 standing in where no reference monitor exists, extending calibration beyond the co-location site.

The four strands feed one engine: blend the evidence, remove the physically explained bias, then let a dynamic model carry the correction across the network as conditions change. The outcome is high-density PM2.5 data with an accuracy that has been verified rather than assumed.

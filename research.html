Research

Five threads, one question: when can you believe a cheap sensor?

Calibration chambers

Sensor evaluation studies rarely agree, largely because they run under conditions nobody else can reproduce. I designed and commissioned a 1.3 m³ environmental chamber with independent control over aerosol source, concentration, temperature and humidity, and used it to set out how low-cost sensors should be assessed — treating number and mass concentration separately, since a sensor can pass on one and fail on the other.

Physical-optical calibration

Most corrections are empirical: fit against a reference and hope conditions hold. They usually don't. I build the correction from the instrument outward instead — Mie scattering through the sensor's own optics and detector response, with humidity handled as κ–Köhler particle growth rather than a regression variable. Grounded in physics, the model doesn't need refitting each time the aerosol changes.

Sensor networks

A city-scale network can't afford a reference monitor at every node, so calibrations must travel. I've examined how far they travel and why they stop, and used MERRA-2 reconstructed PM2.5 as a proxy where no reference existed at all. I also built the IoT sensor network architecture across the IIT Bombay campus.

Source apportionment

Calibration and source attribution are the same problem from opposite ends — both depend on what the particles actually are. I work on receptor modelling from low-cost sensor data, and on machine-learning classification to label the factors that receptor models return.

Measurement for policy

I built the analysis pipeline for a pilot study evaluating Delhi's smog tower at two sites, pairing reference-grade monitors with sensor arrays to test a heavily publicised intervention against measurement. I also contributed to a T20 policy brief for the G20 on low-cost sensor and satellite synergies.
